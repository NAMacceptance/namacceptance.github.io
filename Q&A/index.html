Context

Based on specific TSAR method records and lifecycle-source documents

Evidence note: The answers below are qualitative syntheses of documented TSAR lifecycle events. Where a question asks what occurs 'most often' or 'how often', the available source material does not by itself support a defensible numerical frequency estimate. In those cases, the answer identifies recurring documented patterns without ranking them statistically. A stopped or incomplete lifecycle record is not treated as proof of scientific failure unless the underlying source explicitly documents a causal problem.

1. What factors most often prevent NAMs from progressing beyond the submission stage?
The TSAR records do not support a single ranked cause, but several recurring barriers are documented: incomplete or immature submissions, unresolved requests for additional information, loss of submitter follow-up, uncertainty about regulatory relevance, programme prioritisation decisions, and practical access or intellectual-property constraints. These events should be distinguished from scientific invalidity. For example, the automated zebrafish cardiotoxicity assay stopped after requested additional information was not provided, while CAMVA, VitroDerm and the reconstructed-human-epidermis skin-absorption route were closed after submitter communication ceased. PALM and MELN show that material-transfer arrangements can block progression even when a validation route is otherwise feasible (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2009-07; TM2008-06; TM2009-10; TM2008-13; TM2010-02; TM2009-02).
Evidence examples: TM2009-07 Zebrafish cardiotoxicity assay; TM2008-06 CAMVA; TM2009-10 VitroDerm; TM2008-13 Skin absorption RHE; TM2010-02 PALM; TM2009-02 MELN.
2. How clearly should the intended regulatory use of a NAM be defined before submission?
The intended context of use should be defined as specifically as possible before or during submission. TSAR cases show that scientific plausibility alone may not be enough when the regulatory decision that the method is intended to support remains uncertain. GreenScreen was assessed against several proposed regulatory-use scenarios before the route stopped prior to peer review, while the γH2AX ICW submission was not prioritised when its added value within the existing genotoxicity strategy and the supporting mechanistic information were not sufficiently clear. Developers therefore benefit from specifying whether a NAM is intended for screening, classification, prioritisation, waiving, supporting evidence, an IATA/weight-of-evidence role, or stand-alone use (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2009-08; TM2014-02).
Evidence examples: TM2009-08 GreenScreen HC; TM2014-02 γH2AX ICW.
3. What information is most often missing from NAM submissions?
Recurring requests concern protocol and SOP maturity, supporting datasets, reproducibility information, prediction-model details, performance characteristics, applicability information and clarification of the proposed use. Ocular Irritection required several rounds of additional information and a revised submission, including work around Performance Standards for proprietary elements. STE underwent repeated revisions of its evidence package before it was considered sufficiently mature, and LuSens required repeated presubmission and full-submission refinement. In contrast, SENS-IS ultimately illustrates the consequence of a package whose data quality and reproducibility evidence were not adequate for a transparent and reliable peer review (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2006-02; TM2008-15; TM2011-10; TM2011-11).
Evidence examples: TM2006-02 Ocular Irritection; TM2008-15 STE; TM2011-10 LuSens; TM2011-11 SENS-IS.
4. Which types of technical limitations most commonly delay or prevent further development?
Documented technical barriers include operator-dependent scoring, protocol variability, inadequate early predictive performance, chemical-class-specific false predictions, limited metabolic competence, product- or matrix-specific interference, and problems related to particular physical or chemical properties. These limitations do not necessarily prevent acceptance. EPISKIN initially showed low predictive performance and substantial over-prediction, but protocol and prediction-model optimisation enabled later progression. BCOP and ICE reached regulatory use with explicit chemical-class and classification-scope limitations managed through defined use conditions rather than eliminated entirely (Fentem et al., 2002; OECD, 2013a, 2013b; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2005-04; TM2003-02; TM2003-03).
Evidence examples: TM2005-04 EPISKIN-RHE; TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; Fentem et al. (2002); OECD TG 437 and TG 438.
5. How important is the early definition of a method’s applicability domain?
The evidence indicates that applicability-domain definition is central to regulatory usefulness. Several successful methods achieved acceptance not by demonstrating universal performance but by defining where their results can and cannot be relied upon. Corrositex was accepted with a restricted applicability domain, while BCOP and ICE use explicit interpretation safeguards for chemical classes and classification outcomes that are not handled reliably. This supports defining the intended chemical, mechanistic and decision domain prospectively and refining it as validation evidence accumulates (OECD, 2013a, 2013b; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM1998-01; TM2003-02; TM2003-03).
Evidence examples: TM1998-01 Corrositex; TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; OECD TG 437 and TG 438.
6. What evidence on repeatability, reproducibility and transferability should be generated before formal validation?
Developers should generate evidence on within-laboratory repeatability, between-laboratory reproducibility, operator effects, protocol robustness and transferability to independent laboratories as early as feasible. TSAR validation histories repeatedly show that these are active questions rather than formalities. h-CLAT required transferability and within-/between-laboratory reproducibility confirmation; epiCS required additional training after initial within-laboratory reproducibility problems; and the monocyte activation test variants show that even methods that ultimately achieve acceptance can retain product-specific qualification requirements after generic validation (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2008-05; TM2009-09; TM2002-01–TM2002-05; Hoffmann et al., 2005; Schindler et al., 2006).
Evidence examples: TM2008-05 h-CLAT; TM2009-09 epiCS SIT; TM2002-01 to TM2002-05 monocyte activation tests; Hoffmann et al. (2005); Schindler et al. (2006).
7. When should regulators or validation bodies be involved during method development?
The TSAR trajectories support involving regulatory and validation expertise before major formal validation commitments are made, particularly where the proposed regulatory role is novel or uncertain. Regulatory-relevance consultation occurred for GreenScreen and other methods before later lifecycle stages, and some routes were not prioritised despite scientific plausibility. Early dialogue can identify whether the claimed context of use is meaningful, whether the evidence package is mature enough, and which remaining uncertainties would still block later use (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2009-08; TM2014-02; TM2010-01).
Evidence examples: TM2009-08 GreenScreen HC; TM2014-02 γH2AX ICW; TM2010-01 ARBA.
8. Which characteristics are associated with NAMs that successfully progress to validation?
Across successful examples, progression is associated with a sufficiently stable protocol, interpretable prediction rules, reproducible performance, an evidence-supported applicability domain and a clearly articulated purpose. Importantly, the method does not have to be perfect before validation. EPISKIN, EpiOcular EIT and epiCS all progressed after identifiable technical or reproducibility problems were resolved through optimisation, additional evidence or training (Fentem et al., 2002; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2005-04; TM2008-10; TM2009-09).
Evidence examples: TM2005-04 EPISKIN-RHE; TM2008-10 EpiOcular EIT; TM2009-09 epiCS SIT.
9. How often do methods need protocol revisions or additional data before progressing?
The source material does not justify a numerical percentage, but revision is a recurrent feature of successful as well as unsuccessful routes. EPISKIN required substantial protocol and prediction-model optimisation after weak early performance; EpiOcular EIT required optimisation of the solid-material protocol; Ocular Irritection, STE and LuSens underwent repeated dossier or information revisions. These cases indicate that iterative refinement is a normal part of method maturation and should not automatically be interpreted as failure (Fentem et al., 2002; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2005-04; TM2008-10; TM2006-02; TM2008-15; TM2011-10).
Evidence examples: TM2005-04 EPISKIN-RHE; TM2008-10 EpiOcular EIT; TM2006-02 Ocular Irritection; TM2008-15 STE; TM2011-10 LuSens.
10. When is it more appropriate to position a NAM as part of an IATA or weight-of-evidence approach rather than as a stand-alone replacement?
An integrated role is particularly appropriate when a method captures only part of the relevant biology, has a restricted applicability domain, or cannot reliably support the full regulatory classification spectrum on its own. KeratinoSens can miss some sensitisers that depend on bioactivation or slow pre-hapten oxidation, and its recommended role is therefore within an integrated framework. The 3T3 NRU assays similarly support defined decisions within weight-of-evidence approaches rather than complete replacement of acute-toxicity classification. Eye-irritation defined approaches show how complementary methods can be combined to address individual assay limitations (OECD, 2018, 2022; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2010-03; TM2003-01; TM2007-03; TM2003-02; TM2003-03).
Evidence examples: TM2010-03 KeratinoSens; TM2003-01 NRU cytotoxicity; TM2007-03 3T3 NRU; TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; OECD TG 442D and TG 467.
11. Which methodological limitations are most often successfully resolved during development or validation?
The clearest resolved obstacles involve protocol optimisation, training, operator standardisation, prediction thresholds, additional endpoints and reproducibility. EPISKIN's early over-prediction and low accuracy were addressed through protocol refinement; EpiOcular's solid-material protocol was optimised; epiCS used additional training to address reproducibility; and the WEC embryotoxicity method required alternative endpoints and prediction models after the original total morphological scoring system could not be reproduced reliably. Fundamental biological coverage limitations are more often managed by restricting scope or integrating other evidence rather than eliminated (Fentem et al., 2002; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2005-04; TM2008-10; TM2009-09; TM1999-03).
Evidence examples: TM2005-04 EPISKIN-RHE; TM2008-10 EpiOcular EIT; TM2009-09 epiCS SIT; TM1999-03 WEC.
12. What can developers learn from NAMs that were discontinued but later progressed through another route?
A discontinued TSAR route is not necessarily the end of method development. MUSST was replaced by the revised U-SENS submission; the earlier SkinEthic HCE route was followed by a revised SkinEthic HCE EIT submission; and ERα-CALUX progressed through external validation and later OECD incorporation despite not being prioritised for an EURL ECVAM-coordinated validation study. Method evolution, resubmission and alternative validation or standardisation routes can therefore represent successful learning rather than simple failure (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2009-05; TM2013-02; TM2008-09; TM2015-03; TM2009-11).
Evidence examples: TM2009-05 MUSST → TM2013-02 U-SENS; TM2008-09 SkinEthic HCE → TM2015-03 SkinEthic HCE EIT; TM2009-11 ERα-CALUX.
1. Which issues most commonly emerge during validation and peer review of NAMs?
Recurring documented issues include within- and between-laboratory reproducibility, protocol standardisation, prediction performance, evidence quality, applicability-domain definition, reference-chemical coverage and the clarity of the proposed regulatory role. These issues occur in methods that ultimately succeed as well as those that stop. Validation is therefore best viewed as a structured process for identifying and resolving decision-relevant uncertainty, not simply as a pass/fail gate (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2005-04; TM2008-05; TM2009-09; TM2011-11; TM2004-07).
Evidence examples: TM2005-04 EPISKIN-RHE; TM2008-05 h-CLAT; TM2009-09 epiCS SIT; TM2011-11 SENS-IS; TM2004-07 BALB/c 3T3 CTA.
2. What are the most frequent causes of insufficient reproducibility or transferability?
The source material identifies operator-dependent scoring, training requirements, biological-source variability, incompletely standardised procedures, test-system variability and laboratory-to-laboratory differences as recurrent contributors. For example, epiCS required training after within-laboratory reproducibility problems; BALB/c 3T3 CTA relied on subjective focus scoring and clearer criteria; and the monocyte activation test variants required multi-laboratory evaluation because use of primary cells or whole blood can introduce additional variability (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2009-09; TM2004-07; TM2002-01–TM2002-05; Hoffmann et al., 2005).
Evidence examples: TM2009-09 epiCS SIT; TM2004-07 BALB/c 3T3 CTA; TM2002-01 to TM2002-05 MAT variants.
3. Which types of additional evidence are most often requested during review?
Requests commonly involve additional reproducibility evidence, broader chemical coverage, protocol or prediction-model clarification, data that support the proposed applicability domain, and documentation that allows transparent re-analysis. KeratinoSens required additional within-laboratory reproducibility information; h-CLAT's transferability and reproducibility evidence was central to validation; and SENS-IS peer review ultimately stopped because the quality of submitted data did not permit a transparent, consistent and reliable review (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2010-03; TM2008-05; TM2011-11).
Evidence examples: TM2010-03 KeratinoSens; TM2008-05 h-CLAT; TM2011-11 SENS-IS.
4. How often are applicability-domain limitations identified during validation rather than during development?
The available evidence does not support a numerical estimate, but several method histories show the applicability domain becoming more precise during validation and review as broader chemical sets reveal class-specific performance. BCOP and ICE illustrate chemical-class-dependent prediction errors; Corrositex was ultimately accepted only for certain chemical types; and ZFET retained important case-dependent limitations despite successful reproducibility work and OECD standardisation. Applicability should therefore be treated as something that is progressively characterised, not assumed to be fixed at initial development (OECD, 2013a, 2013b; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM1998-01; TM2003-02; TM2003-03; TM2006-01).
Evidence examples: TM1998-01 Corrositex; TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; TM2006-01 ZFET.
5. Which limitations can usually be resolved through protocol refinement, and which tend to remain?
Limitations linked to exposure conditions, assay timing, scoring rules, operator training, protocol harmonisation and prediction thresholds are often amenable to refinement. EPISKIN, EpiOcular EIT and epiCS provide examples. By contrast, missing metabolic competence, restricted biological-mechanism coverage, inability to determine reversibility, or inability to resolve all regulatory categories tend to remain as design limitations. These are better managed through defined applicability, complementary evidence or integrated approaches than by assuming that further protocol optimisation will remove them (Fentem et al., 2002; OECD, 2013a, 2022; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2005-04; TM2008-10; TM2009-09; TM2010-03; TM2003-03).
Evidence examples: TM2005-04 EPISKIN-RHE; TM2008-10 EpiOcular EIT; TM2009-09 epiCS; TM2010-03 KeratinoSens; TM2003-03 BCOP.
6. How should reviewers distinguish between a fundamental method limitation and a correctable validation issue?
A correctable validation issue is one for which protocol changes, training, additional data or harmonisation can plausibly restore reliable performance. A fundamental limitation is rooted in what the biological or technological system can represent. EPISKIN's early predictive problem proved correctable through optimisation, whereas KeratinoSens' reduced ability to detect some bioactivation-dependent sensitisers reflects mechanistic coverage and is therefore managed through integration with other evidence. BCOP's inability to determine reversibility is similarly a design limitation rather than a simple validation defect (Fentem et al., 2002; OECD, 2013a, 2018; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2005-04; TM2010-03; TM2003-03).
Evidence examples: TM2005-04 EPISKIN-RHE; TM2010-03 KeratinoSens; TM2003-03 BCOP.
7. Which performance characteristics appear most important for successful progression to regulatory consideration?
The TSAR cases repeatedly emphasise reproducibility, transferability, sensitivity and specificity in the intended context, known false-positive and false-negative patterns, protocol robustness and a clearly bounded applicability domain. Reviewers should therefore look beyond a single global accuracy statistic. In BCOP, for example, regulatory interpretation depends materially on known class-specific over- and under-prediction. In the monocyte activation tests, generic validity coexists with product-specific suitability requirements (OECD, 2013a; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2003-03; TM2002-01–TM2002-05).
Evidence examples: TM2003-03 BCOP; TM2002-01 to TM2002-05 MAT variants.
8. How often does scientific validation result in regulatory uptake?
The source material does not justify a single conversion percentage, and the method histories show clearly that scientific validation does not guarantee regulatory uptake. Cytosensor completed validation and peer review and entered OECD Test Guideline development, but the guideline project was stopped because the validated technology was no longer commercially available. Cryopreserved human hepatocytes progressed through validation and peer review but OECD later selected the related HepaRG approach for guideline development. These are late-stage implementation and standards-route barriers rather than failures of scientific validation (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2004-01; TM2009-13; TM2009-14).
Evidence examples: TM2004-01 Cytosensor; TM2009-13 CryoHeps; TM2009-14 HepaRG.
9. At what stage should the intended regulatory context of use be reassessed?
The intended context of use should be revisited before validation, during peer review and again before standards development or implementation. As evidence accumulates, the defensible use may become narrower, more conditional or more integrated than initially proposed. The 3T3 NRU assays, BCOP, ICE and several embryotoxicity methods show that useful regulatory roles can remain even when the evidence does not support broad one-to-one replacement of the legacy animal test (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2003-01; TM2007-03; TM2003-02; TM2003-03; TM1999-01–TM1999-03).
Evidence examples: TM2003-01 NRU cytotoxicity; TM2007-03 3T3 NRU; TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; TM1999-01 to TM1999-03 embryotoxicity methods.
10. When is validation of an integrated approach more appropriate than validation of a single NAM in isolation?
An integrated approach is particularly appropriate when the regulatory decision depends on multiple biological events or evidence streams that no single assay can capture adequately. The OECD defined approaches for serious eye damage and eye irritation combine complementary information sources to manage limitations of individual assays such as BCOP and ICE. Skin-sensitisation methods such as KeratinoSens likewise have intended roles within an IATA because mechanistic coverage is incomplete at the single-assay level (OECD, 2018, 2022; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2003-02; TM2003-03; TM2010-03).
Evidence examples: TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; TM2010-03 KeratinoSens; OECD TG 442D and TG 467.
11. Which bottlenecks identified during peer review most often delay subsequent regulatory acceptance?
The evidence identifies insufficient evidence for the claimed regulatory use, incomplete reproducibility evidence, unresolved applicability questions, absence of adequate performance standards and uncertainty about how the method fits existing decision frameworks as recurring late-stage issues. Neutral Red Release and RBC haemolysis completed retrospective validation, but ESAC concluded that the evidence was insufficient to support regulatory recommendation. BALB/c 3T3 CTA required further assessment criteria, procedure refinement and supporting evidence before broader regulatory use could be considered (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2004-03; TM2004-04; TM2004-07).
Evidence examples: TM2004-03 Neutral Red Release; TM2004-04 RBC haemolysis; TM2004-07 BALB/c 3T3 CTA.
12. What lessons can be learned from successfully validated NAMs that nevertheless experienced limited regulatory uptake?
Scientific validity and regulatory acceptability answer different questions. A method may be reproducible and scientifically valid but still lack a sufficiently useful regulatory role, practical implementation route, commercial availability or standards pathway. Cytosensor and CryoHeps illustrate post-validation barriers, while the embryotoxicity methods EST, Micromass and WEC were considered scientifically validated but not replacements for reproductive-toxicity testing as a whole. Review should therefore assess both scientific confidence and decision relevance explicitly (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2004-01; TM2009-13; TM1999-01–TM1999-03).
Evidence examples: TM2004-01 Cytosensor; TM2009-13 CryoHeps; TM1999-01 EST; TM1999-02 Micromass; TM1999-03 WEC.
1. Which factors most commonly prevent scientifically promising NAMs from progressing to regulatory acceptance?
The TSAR records do not support a single ranked factor, but recurring barriers include unclear regulatory purpose, restricted or insufficiently characterised applicability, incomplete evidence for the proposed decision, dependence on complementary evidence, standards-development barriers and practical implementation constraints. Cytosensor shows that commercial availability can stop a standards route after scientific validation, while GreenScreen illustrates how unresolved regulatory relevance can prevent progression before peer review (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2004-01; TM2009-08).
Evidence examples: TM2004-01 Cytosensor; TM2009-08 GreenScreen HC; TM2009-13 CryoHeps; TM2009-14 HepaRG.
2. At what stage does regulatory uncertainty most often become a bottleneck?
Regulatory uncertainty can emerge early during presubmission or submission assessment and can reappear after successful scientific validation. Early in the lifecycle, the central question may be whether the proposed use addresses a regulatory need at all; later, the question becomes how validated results can be incorporated into an existing decision framework. GreenScreen and γH2AX ICW show early regulatory-relevance uncertainty, while ZFET demonstrates that OECD standardisation does not automatically settle every sector-specific replacement question (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2009-08; TM2014-02; TM2006-01).
Evidence examples: TM2009-08 GreenScreen HC; TM2014-02 γH2AX ICW; TM2006-01 ZFET.
3. How important is early regulatory consultation for successful NAM progression?
Early consultation is valuable because it can identify unsupported use claims, unclear contexts of use and missing decision-relevant evidence before a developer commits to a large validation programme. TSAR contains cases in which PARERE or other regulatory bodies were consulted on proposed uses before later stages. Such consultation cannot guarantee acceptance, but it can reveal whether a technically successful validation would still fail to answer the intended regulatory question (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2009-08; TM2014-04; TM2026-01).
Evidence examples: TM2009-08 GreenScreen HC; TM2014-04 devTOXqP; TM2026-01 Lung Surfactant Bioassay.
4. Which types of NAMs are more often accepted as stand-alone methods, and which are used as part of integrated approaches?
The source material suggests that stand-alone acceptance is most feasible when a method can support a clearly bounded regulatory conclusion with acceptable reliability. Methods that capture only one mechanism or part of a classification spectrum are more often positioned as supporting components. BCOP and ICE can support specified eye-damage or non-classification decisions under defined conditions, while KeratinoSens and mechanistic endocrine assays are intended to contribute to broader integrated evidence rather than provide complete apical hazard conclusions on their own (OECD, 2013a, 2013b, 2018, 2022; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2003-02; TM2003-03; TM2010-03; TM2008-17; TM2010-07).
Evidence examples: TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; TM2010-03 KeratinoSens; TM2008-17 ER-STTA; TM2010-07 AR-CALUX.
5. How often does OECD or other formal standardisation lead to practical regulatory uptake?
Formal standardisation is an important milestone but does not by itself establish universal regulatory sufficiency across sectors. ZFET reached OECD TG 236, yet later analysis under REACH did not support automatic one-to-one replacement of the adult fish acute toxicity test. Conversely, some methods can achieve a useful supporting role in guidance without becoming a complete standalone replacement. Standardisation should therefore be distinguished from sector-specific acceptance and implementation (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2006-01; OECD, 2013c).
Evidence examples: TM2006-01 ZFET; OECD TG 236.
6. Which applicability-domain limitations most often restrict regulatory use?
Recurring restrictions involve chemical classes, physicochemical properties, lack of metabolic activation, product or matrix interference, restricted biological mechanisms and inability to determine all classification categories. BCOP and ICE show class-dependent prediction biases; ZFET can be affected by chorion permeability, metabolic differences, low solubility, volatility and adsorption; and the monocyte activation tests require product-specific validation because matrix effects can interfere with the measurement (OECD, 2013a, 2013b, 2013c; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2002-01–TM2002-05; TM2003-02; TM2003-03; TM2006-01).
Evidence examples: TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; TM2006-01 ZFET; TM2002-01 to TM2002-05 MAT variants.
7. What kinds of remaining uncertainty are compatible with regulatory use, and which tend to prevent acceptance?
Uncertainty can be compatible with regulatory use when it is characterised, bounded and managed by applicability restrictions, decision rules, follow-up testing or complementary evidence. BCOP and ICE are examples of accepted methods whose limitations are explicitly incorporated into how results are interpreted. In contrast, uncertainty becomes much more problematic when the evidence package itself does not permit a transparent and reliable assessment, as occurred in SENS-IS peer review. The distinction is therefore between known/manageable uncertainty and uncertainty arising from inadequate evidence quality (OECD, 2013a, 2013b; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2003-02; TM2003-03; TM2011-11).
Evidence examples: TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; TM2011-11 SENS-IS.
8. Which evidence gaps most often remain after scientific validation?
Residual gaps often concern performance in less-represented chemical classes, product-specific matrices, specific regulatory categories, unusual mechanisms or broader contexts of use beyond the original validation set. The monocyte activation tests demonstrate that generic validation can coexist with a continuing requirement for product-specific suitability. ZFET required continuing attention to paired fish/FET data and case-dependent limitations, while the embryotoxicity methods retained uncertainty for weak or non-embryotoxic chemicals despite reproducible validation studies (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2002-01–TM2002-05; TM2006-01; TM1999-01–TM1999-03).
Evidence examples: TM2002-01 to TM2002-05 MAT variants; TM2006-01 ZFET; TM1999-01 EST; TM1999-02 Micromass; TM1999-03 WEC.
9. How can regulators identify opportunities for conditional or context-specific acceptance rather than requiring full replacement?
A practical approach is to ask which specific regulatory conclusion the NAM can support reliably and under what conditions. Corrositex, BCOP and ICE demonstrate that restricted applicability or sequential testing can enable useful regulatory roles even when a method does not reproduce the full legacy test. The OECD defined approaches for eye damage/irritation make this principle explicit by combining complementary information sources and fixed interpretation procedures (OECD, 2013a, 2013b, 2022; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM1998-01; TM2003-02; TM2003-03).
Evidence examples: TM1998-01 Corrositex; TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; OECD TG 467.
10. Which NAMs have progressed successfully through alternative regulatory or standardisation routes, and what can be learned from them?
Several TSAR histories show that a method can progress outside the initially expected EURL ECVAM route. ERα-CALUX was not prioritised for a coordinated EURL ECVAM validation but progressed through external validation and later OECD incorporation. RTgill-W1 stopped in TSAR submission but continued externally and subsequently reached ISO standardisation and OECD TG 249. These cases show that route selection and programme prioritisation should not be equated with scientific merit, and that alternative international routes can sometimes be more appropriate (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2009-11; TM2014-01).
Evidence examples: TM2009-11 ERα-CALUX; TM2014-01 RTgill-W1.
11. Where does the largest delay typically occur between validation, recommendation, guideline development and implementation?
The evidence base does not support identifying one statistically dominant delay stage. It does, however, document substantial late-stage delays and interruptions during international standards development and implementation. Cytosensor's OECD project stopped after validation because the technology was unavailable; HepaRG required additional applicability-expansion work during a prolonged OECD guideline route; and early phototoxicity and replacement trajectories show that scientific acceptance and formal standards adoption can be separated by many years. International standardisation should therefore be treated as its own lifecycle risk (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2004-01; TM2009-14; TM1994-01; TM1997-01).
Evidence examples: TM2004-01 Cytosensor; TM2009-14 HepaRG; TM1994-01 3T3 NRU Phototoxicity; TM1997-01 TABST.
12. What characteristics distinguish NAMs that achieve regulatory uptake from those that stop after validation?
Successful regulatory uptake appears to require alignment among scientific reliability, reproducibility, applicability, regulatory relevance, implementability and a viable guidance or standards route. A method may retain limitations and still be accepted if those limitations are characterised and incorporated into the conditions of use. Conversely, scientifically credible methods may stall when practical access, commercial availability, regulatory purpose or standards prioritisation remains unresolved. The decisive issue is therefore not the absence of limitations but whether the remaining uncertainty is compatible with a defined regulatory decision (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2003-02; TM2003-03; TM2004-01; TM2009-13; TM2009-14).
Evidence examples: TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; TM2004-01 Cytosensor; TM2009-13 CryoHeps; TM2009-14 HepaRG.
1. Where in the NAM lifecycle do bottlenecks occur most frequently?
A defensible frequency ranking requires a dedicated quantitative event-level analysis and cannot be inferred from lifecycle status alone. Qualitatively, the source records show substantial numbers of submission/readiness events, but important obstacles also occur during validation, peer review, international standardisation and implementation. The core lesson is that bottlenecks are lifecycle-specific and include scientific, documentation, regulatory, organisational and implementation problems (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TSAR method records cited throughout this document).
Evidence examples: Submission examples: TM2009-07, TM2008-06, TM2009-10. Validation examples: TM2009-09, TM2008-09. Late-stage examples: TM2004-01, TM2009-13, TM2009-14.
2. Which bottlenecks are most often successfully resolved?
The source histories repeatedly document successful resolution of protocol optimisation, training, reproducibility, prediction-model refinement, broader testing and dossier maturation. EPISKIN, EpiOcular EIT, epiCS and h-CLAT all progressed after such issues were addressed. By contrast, fundamental mechanistic or endpoint-coverage limitations are more often managed by applicability restrictions or integration than fully eliminated (Fentem et al., 2002; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2005-04; TM2008-10; TM2009-09; TM2008-05; TM2010-03).
Evidence examples: TM2005-04 EPISKIN-RHE; TM2008-10 EpiOcular EIT; TM2009-09 epiCS; TM2008-05 h-CLAT; TM2010-03 KeratinoSens.
3. Which bottlenecks are most strongly associated with discontinued methods?
The discontinued records include multiple distinct causes rather than one failure mechanism: submitter non-response, incomplete evidence delivery, non-prioritisation, material-transfer or IPR problems, insufficient peer-review evidence, commercial unavailability, supersession by a revised method and discontinued standards projects. A 'stopped' status should therefore never be interpreted automatically as scientific invalidity (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2008-06; TM2009-07; TM2010-02; TM2011-11; TM2004-01; TM2009-05).
Evidence examples: TM2008-06 CAMVA; TM2009-07 Zebrafish cardiotoxicity; TM2010-02 PALM; TM2011-11 SENS-IS; TM2004-01 Cytosensor; TM2009-05 MUSST.
4. Which factors are most strongly associated with successful regulatory uptake?
The source material supports a recurring combination rather than a single factor: reproducible and transferable performance, a clear protocol, a defined and defensible context of use, explicit applicability boundaries, sufficient decision-relevant evidence and a feasible implementation or standardisation pathway. Successful methods often retain limitations; what distinguishes them is that those limitations are understood and incorporated into the regulatory conditions of use (OECD, 2013a, 2013b, 2018, 2022; EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2003-02; TM2003-03; TM2010-03).
Evidence examples: TM2003-02 ICE; TM2003-03 BCOP; TM2010-03 KeratinoSens; OECD TG 437, TG 438, TG 442D and TG 467.
5. At which stages would earlier communication between developers, reviewers and regulators have the greatest potential to accelerate progress?
The strongest opportunities are before formal submission, before major validation commitments and before international guideline development. At those points, communication can clarify whether the dossier is sufficiently mature, whether the proposed context of use meets a regulatory need, what evidence is required for review, and whether access or implementation constraints could later prevent uptake. GreenScreen, Ocular Irritection, PALM and Cytosensor illustrate why scientific, regulatory and implementation questions should be surfaced before they become late-stage blockers (EURL ECVAM, n.d., TM2009-08; TM2006-02; TM2010-02; TM2004-01).
Evidence examples: TM2009-08 GreenScreen HC; TM2006-02 Ocular Irritection; TM2010-02 PALM; TM2004-01 Cytosensor.
Across the cited TSAR method histories, NAM progression is not a simple linear sequence in which scientific validation automatically leads to regulatory acceptance. Progress depends on alignment among scientific reliability, documentation quality, reproducibility and transferability, applicability, regulatory purpose, implementation feasibility and the selected validation or standardisation route. A limitation is not necessarily a failure: many successful NAMs reached regulatory use by refining protocols, restricting applicability, adding complementary evidence or becoming part of an integrated or defined approach.
